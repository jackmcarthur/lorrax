# 4.4 Integrating over time: the working picture

Section 4.2 replaced energy denominators with integrals like
$\int_0^\infty d\tau\, e^{-x\tau}$ and asserted they can be done with a dozen nodes.
This section explains *why*, at the level a user needs — the quantitative machinery,
with its error laws and window-routing tables, is Chapter 7. Nothing here requires
prior acquaintance with numerical quadrature.

**One decaying exponential is easy; a range of them is the problem.** If every
denominator had the same value $x$, one sample of $e^{-x\tau}$ would do. In reality
$x = \varepsilon_c - \varepsilon_v$ ranges from the band gap (slow decay, long times
matter) up to the full band width (fast decay, only short times matter) — three or
four decades. A single set of nodes $\{t_l, w_l\}$ must represent $1/x$ **uniformly
well for every $x$ in the range at once**. Such optimal shared nodes exist and are
called *minimax* rules: they minimize the worst-case error over the whole interval,
and their count grows only with the *logarithm* of the range ratio
$R = x_{max}/x_{min}$. That logarithm is why $N_\tau \sim O(N^0)$: adding atoms adds
denominators, but barely stretches the range they live in.

**Two kinds of denominators, two time axes.**

- **Noncrossing** denominators keep one sign: in $\chi^0$, every
  $\varepsilon_c - \varepsilon_v$ is positive (nothing crosses the gap). Then
  $e^{-x\tau}$ decays for all terms and the integral lives comfortably on the
  **imaginary-time axis** — smooth, exponentially convergent, the friendly case.
- **Crossing** denominators change sign across the range: in the self-energy,
  combinations like $\omega - \varepsilon_c - \Omega$ pass through zero as the
  evaluation frequency $\omega$ sweeps the quasiparticle range. A decaying-exponential
  representation is impossible ($e^{-x\tau}$ explodes for $x<0$), so these terms are
  handled on the **real-time axis**, where the integrand *oscillates* instead of
  decaying: $1/x$ is represented by a sum of sinusoids
  $\sum_l w_l \sin(x\,\tau_l)$.

**Why oscillatory representation works: bandwidth limiting.** A finite sum of
sinusoids is a bandwidth-limited function — it simply cannot reproduce $1/x$
arbitrarily close to the singularity at $x=0$, nor track it over an unbounded range.
It *can* reproduce $1/x$ on an annulus $x_{min} \le |x| \le x_{max}$. The upper edge
is physics: the band width. The lower edge differs by regime — for noncrossing
denominators the gap supplies it for free, but for crossing denominators $x$
genuinely passes through zero at a resonance, and the floor is an **imposed
broadening** $\eta$ (the finite quasiparticle-linewidth regularization,
`sigma_regularization_ev`, which Chapter 7 exposes as a physical input, not a
numerical trick). LORRAX therefore **sorts denominators into windows of bounded
range before integrating** — each window gets a quadrature rule tuned to its own
$[x_{min}, x_{max}]$. Noncrossing rules stay logarithmically small; crossing rules
are the expensive ones, growing roughly linearly in the bandwidth-to-broadening
ratio (tens up to on the order of a hundred nodes). The window decomposition is what
keeps each rule as small as it can be. This "bandwidth-limit first, integrate second"
step is the single most important idea in the frequency machinery; Chapter 7 does it
quantitatively.

**The one knob.** All of it — node placement, weights, window edges — is derived
automatically from the spectrum and from a single input, `minimax_target_error`, the
worst-case error the user is willing to accept in any represented denominator.

A fair summary of this section for the impatient: *gapped things are integrated on the
imaginary time axis, sign-changing things on the real time axis, both after an
energy-windowing step that makes small optimal quadratures possible, and one input key
controls every error.* A user who remembers that sentence can operate the code
correctly; a user who wants the proofs will find them in Chapter 7 and Kim, Martyna &
Ismail-Beigi, PRB **101**, 035139 (2020).
